The Orchard of Second Moons

On the evening of July 27, 2026, Mara found a second moon growing in the pear tree behind her house. It was small as a saucer and covered in silver fur. When she touched it, all the clocks in the village coughed once and began counting backward.

Her brother Emil had been dead for nine years, but at seven minutes before midnight he knocked on the kitchen window, wearing the blue coat in which they had buried him. Rain streamed upward from his shoulders into the clouds.

“You left the gate open,” he said.

Mara looked toward the orchard. Between the trees, pale animals were entering from the road: a horse with drawers instead of ribs, three eyeless dogs carrying candles in their mouths, and a woman whose long black hair was braided to the horizon. Wherever their feet touched the grass, childhood rooms appeared for an instant—wallpaper, warm lamps, beds still dented by forgotten sleepers—then folded shut like paper fans.

Emil placed the little moon on the table and cut it with their mother’s bread knife. Inside were hundreds of sleeping moths and a red staircase descending farther than the earth should allow.

“I came to return your shadow,” he said.

Only then did Mara notice the shadow draped over his arm. It was younger than she was and trembling. She tried it on, but it remembered another body. Her hands smelled of school chalk; her knees bore summer scabs; behind her teeth waited a song she had refused to sing at their mother’s funeral.

Outside, the clocks reached zero. The village vanished without noise, leaving its doors standing upright in the fields. One by one, the pale animals chose a door and passed through.

Emil stepped onto the red staircase. Mara wanted to follow, but her borrowed shadow held her by the ankles.

“Will morning come?” she asked.

He smiled as he descended. “It has already come. It simply cannot find us.”

At dawn, the pear tree was empty. Mara closed the gate and discovered a warm brass key beneath her tongue. All day she carried it there, afraid that if she spoke, some distant door would open and the sea would walk in.
