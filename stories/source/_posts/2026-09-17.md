At seven each morning, Mara crossed Bellwether Park to adjust the public thermostat. It stood beside the fountain on a brass stalk, its glass dial large as a dinner plate. When she turned it clockwise, summer entered through the east gate; counterclockwise brought a crisp parade of yellow leaves. The city trusted her to keep the park at twenty-two degrees.

One Tuesday, the thermostat refused her hand. Its needle pointed not to a number but to a small painted cup. At once, the paths softened into clay. Joggers sank ankle-deep, laughing nervously, while pigeons left neat three-pronged signatures behind them. From beneath the bandstand came the steady whir of a pottery wheel.

Mara followed the sound underground and found an old potter, Ivo, shaping the park on a spinning table. The miniature trees trembled beneath his wet fingers. “The temperature has cracked,” he said, showing her a hairline fissure across the little fountain. “Heat escapes through form. Cold enters through names.”

He asked Mara to mend it. She pinched clay from the miniature lawn, rolled it into a thin snake, and pressed it over the crack. Above them, benches lengthened like warm bread; swings curled into question marks. The repair would not hold. The clay kept whispering the names of weather: drizzle, glare, thaw, cinnamon.

“Cinnamon isn’t weather,” Mara said. “It is in certain parks.” Together they carried the tiny fountain upstairs. Mara placed it inside the thermostat’s brass casing, where it began to splash backward. Each drop climbed, struck the needle, and became a blue ceramic bird. Soon the dial was crowded with wings, and the park’s temperature fluttered wildly between scarf and lemonade.

Mara understood then that the city had mistaken comfort for stillness. She opened the thermostat and released the birds. They perched along the paths, glazing everything they touched: the pond turned cobalt, the roses shone like red bowls, and every dog acquired a porcelain bark. Ivo rolled up the softened path and tucked it beneath his arm.

By noon, the park had settled at no temperature anyone could measure. Visitors learned to dress in layers and carry teaspoons. Mara resigned her official key but kept one blue bird in her pocket. Whenever the city grew too certain, it tapped against her hip, and somewhere a cup appeared, already full of wind.
