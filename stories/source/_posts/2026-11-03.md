At noon, the restaurant unfolded from a napkin in the middle of Bellweather Square. Its walls rose like pleated sleeves, its windows blinked awake, and twelve tables shook crumbs from their shoulders. Mina, who had come only to mail a letter, found herself seated beneath a chandelier of silver spoons. Her envelope was taken for a menu.

The waiter wore roller skates and the expression of someone balancing a secret on his tongue. “Today,” he said, “we recommend the witty soup. It answers before you ask.” He circled her chair with such graceful speed that the salt leapt into the pepper shaker for safety. Mina ordered water. The waiter bowed. “Still or opinionated?”

Before she could decide, the kitchen doors swung open and released a procession of plates. One carried a tiny thunderstorm; another held three blue keys arguing about which lock deserved them. The last bore Mina’s unmailed letter, sliced neatly into sentences and garnished with parsley. Each sentence wriggled toward the table’s edge.

“That is private,” Mina said. “Everything becomes public when served with parsley,” replied the waiter. “House policy.” Mina snatched at the first sentence, but it dodged her fingers and read itself aloud: Dear Mayor, the pigeons have begun charging rent.

The restaurant fell silent. Outside, pigeons were indeed measuring the square with tape. Diners checked their pockets. A woman discovered a nest where her wallet had been. The waiter whispered, “Your letter appears to be today’s special.”

Mina stood. She had never danced, yet the floor tilted politely beneath her shoes. She glided between tables, collecting her sentences as they sprang from plates, teacups, and startled mouths. The waiter pursued her, skating elegant loops and tossing witty objections like confetti: “Madam, kidnapping an entrée is considered poor tipping!” She caught each sentence and pressed it back onto the envelope, though the words returned in a different order.

At the door, she read the new letter: Dear Pigeons, the mayor has begun charging restaurants rent.

The building trembled. The tables folded into napkins; the kitchen shrank to a warm pocket in the pavement. Mina stepped aside with a graceful bow as the waiter rolled past, still holding her opinionated water.

By evening, the pigeons owned the square. They hired Mina as their accountant and opened a restaurant where every bill told a joke. No one laughed until they had paid.
