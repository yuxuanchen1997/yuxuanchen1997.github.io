title: "The Smallest Hour"

Every morning, the town of Bellweather arrived five minutes early. Doors opened before hands touched their knobs, kettles sang over unlit stoves, and apologies were accepted before anyone had spoken. Only Mara, keeper of the public clock, remained on ordinary time. She slept in the clock tower with a white kitten curled in her toolbox and woke each day to find tomorrow’s dust on its paws.

One dawn, the kitten stole Mara’s smallest mallet and climbed inside the clock. Instead of gears, Mara found a narrow kitchen where twelve identical bakers kneaded a single ribbon of blue dough. At every strike of the mallet, the dough became an hour: warm, fragrant, and slightly impatient. The kitten struck twice. Outside, Wednesday leaped over Thursday and landed badly in Saturday.

The town erupted into premature picnics. Shopkeepers sold receipts for purchases not yet desired. Children returned from school carrying assignments their teachers had not invented. In the square, a fountain poured upward, collecting coins from startled pockets. Everyone begged Mara to restore the missing days, though several admitted Saturday suited them.

Mara followed the kitten across the floury table. “Early is not the same as ready,” she told it. The kitten blinked, which was its method of disagreeing with architecture, weather, and advice. It tapped the mallet once more. A tiny hour sprang from the dough, bit Mara’s sleeve, and hid in her pocket.

Then Mara understood. Time had not been running fast; it had been escaping, frightened by the town’s hunger to finish everything before it began. She took the mallet from the kitten and struck the table softly, not like a judge, but like someone knocking at a neighbor’s door.

The twelve bakers paused. One by one, they folded Thursday back into the dough and shaped it again, leaving a thumbprint in every hour. When Mara descended, Bellweather was exactly nowhere in particular: shops half-open, toast cooling, sentences waiting for their final words. The townspeople discovered they could breathe without arriving anywhere.

As for the kitten, it kept the smallest hour beneath its tongue. Sometimes, just before breakfast, Mara heard it purring—a sound that made the clock hesitate, and the whole town deliciously late.
