At sunrise, Farmer Iven found a wooden printing press growing between two rows of cabbages. Its handles were green, its screws smelled of rain, and beneath its platen lay a single sheet of warm paper. Iven had planted beans there, but the beans now climbed the press and hung from it like commas waiting for a sentence.

He pulled the lever. The press sighed and printed an address: TO THE FARMER WHO HAS FORGOTTEN HIS SECOND FIELD. Iven owned only one field, unless the narrow strip of blue behind the barn counted. He had always assumed it was sky, though sometimes a scarecrow waded through it and returned with minnows in its pockets.

By noon, a package arrived without messenger or road. It was wrapped in brown paper printed with tiny weather reports, all describing conditions inside the box: scattered spoons, rising bread, a strong chance of yellow. When Iven lifted it, every cabbage leaned closer. When he shook it, the barn door opened onto a room he had never built.

Inside, shelves held hundreds of packages, each bearing his name in a different handwriting. He opened one and released the sound of his boots crossing a winter kitchen. Another contained six red buttons and the patience to sew them on. A third was full of damp soil. From it sprouted a small version of the printing press, already busy making labels.

Iven carried the first package to the blue strip and pressed it into the surface. The sky dented like dough. He pressed harder until the paper split, and an entire field unfolded: wheat with glass stems, furrows humming in harmony, a well whose bucket brought up afternoons. At the center stood another Iven, waving with both hands because neither of them knew which was the original.

Together they returned to the press. One fed it blank paper while the other turned the handle. All evening it printed packages flat as maps, each addressed to a farmer somewhere impossible. They planted them under the cabbages. By morning, the field was covered in small brown doors, knocking softly from below.
