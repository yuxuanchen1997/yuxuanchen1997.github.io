Every morning, Mara, the archivist of Low Water, unlocked a library that had no walls. Its shelves rose directly from the marsh on stilts thin as knitting needles, and the books slept in waxed boxes above the reeds. Beneath them, minnows arranged themselves into commas, correcting the current.

Her duty was to catalogue the berries that appeared each night on the marsh’s floating islands. No two were alike. One tasted of a door someone had almost opened; another contained the sound of yellow cloth being folded. Mara pressed each berry onto a card, where its juice formed a small, legible map. By noon, the map always pointed elsewhere.

For seven seasons, all arrows led harmlessly to abandoned boots, warm stones, or patches of water that reflected kitchens instead of sky. Then a white berry arrived, hard as a button. When Mara crushed it, the card displayed her own handwriting: RETURN WHAT THE MARSH HAS NOT YET GIVEN YOU.

She searched the catalogue. Tomorrow’s pages were already full. They described a red canoe tied to her desk, a rainstorm rising upward from the mud, and an unnamed visitor carrying a jar of dry marsh. At the final entry, the ink became berry seeds. They wriggled into the binding and shut the book around themselves.

Precisely at dusk, the visitor came. It was a child with pockets full of teaspoons. Without speaking, the child opened the jar. A square of dry ground unfolded across the water, complete with a path, a bench, and several patient ants. The marsh recoiled to the horizon, taking the library’s reflections with it.

“You borrowed this,” the child said, handing Mara an empty card. Mara understood. Years ago, before becoming an archivist, she had imagined the marsh, and the marsh had been supplying evidence ever since. She wrote her name on the card and dropped it into the jar. At once, water returned, gently misfiling everything. The shelves became flocks of narrow birds. The catalogue sprouted leaves. Each berry opened a tiny, ink-stained eye.

Only Mara remained unchanged, which frightened her until she noticed a new island drifting near. On it stood a desk, and behind the desk sat the marsh, wearing her spectacles, carefully cataloguing her.
