title: "The Market of Borrowed Light"

At noon, when the streets folded themselves into paper fans, Mara found a market beneath the municipal fountain. The stalls stood ankle-deep in blue water, and every awning dripped upward. At the farthest stall, a merchant in a coat sewn with tiny pockets displayed no fruit, cloth, or spice. He sold glasswork: transparent spoons that stirred forgotten thoughts, bottles with staircases inside them, and delicate birds whose wings chimed without moving.

Mara asked the price of a small glasswork orchard, each tree bearing a single clear pear. “Its price is your sheen,” said the merchant. She laughed, believing sheen belonged to satin and beetles, not people. But he lifted a silver brush and swept it once across her sleeve. A pale radiance came away like dust. At once, the whole market brightened.

She carried the orchard home. Wherever she passed, windows dulled, puddles turned matte, and polished shoes forgot the sky. Her own face in shopfronts looked unfinished, as though someone had erased the final sentence. Yet the orchard in her hands grew dazzling. The pears filled with miniature afternoons, all honey-colored roads and children rolling hoops toward impossible hills.

By evening, the city had lost every glimmer. Bakers could not tell sugar from salt. Bus drivers stopped because the roads no longer knew where to bend. Mara understood that the merchant had taken more than her brightness: sheen was the thin agreement by which surfaces admitted they shared one world. Without it, each object became a stubborn country.

She returned and smashed the glasswork orchard against the fountain. It did not break. Instead, it unfolded root by root, becoming a transparent forest under the water. Mara climbed among its branches and plucked the brightest pear. Inside, the stolen sheen trembled, mixed with thousands of others. She bit the fruit. Light flooded her mouth, tasting of coins, raincoats, teacups, and names spoken kindly.

The radiance rushed back through the streets. The merchant’s coat became plain, its pockets full of water. He bowed, neither defeated nor pleased. “Every city must discover what holds it together,” he said. Then he stepped into a bottle and pulled the cork above him. Mara left the orchard growing, so anyone could see how easily the world might come apart—and how strangely it could be mended.
