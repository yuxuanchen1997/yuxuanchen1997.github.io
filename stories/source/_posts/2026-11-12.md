title: "The Road in the Tin"
date: 2026-11-12 00:00:00

Every Tuesday, Mara drove a blue van along a road that appeared only after breakfast. It began behind the bakery, crossed three empty lots, and ended wherever the van decided to stop. In the back, she carried hundreds of tin boxes. Each contained a small, neatly folded sound: a kettle beginning to sing, a shoe turning in sleep, rain remembering a roof.

Her duty was to deliver the sounds to houses that had misplaced them. She never knocked. Beside every door was a round hole exactly the width of her wooden dowel. Mara inserted the dowel, listened through its grain, and learned which tin belonged there. The wood might whisper “spoon against bowl” or “green bicycle bell.” Then she opened the chosen box, and the sound slipped under the door.

One Tuesday, the road curled into the van itself.

Mara braked, but the painted white line continued beneath her seat and vanished among the tins. From the cargo space came a steady tapping. One box had grown to the size of a cupboard, and in its lid was a round hole. The dowel trembled in her hand.

She pushed it in.

Through the wood, Mara heard the van driving without her: tires humming over blue gravel, mirrors blinking at fields of upright fish, the horn politely asking mountains to move aside. Beneath these noises waited another sound—her own kitchen chair scraping backward, though she had left no one at home.

Mara opened the enormous tin. The van folded inward like a paper map. Road, boxes, steering wheel, and morning all slid into the shining container. She stood in her kitchen, holding the dowel, while the chair finished moving by itself.

On the table sat a tiny blue van. Its engine was running. Behind its windshield, a woman no larger than a thimble raised a wooden dowel and listened to Mara through the glass.

Neither of them moved. From somewhere inside the wall came the soft metallic click of a tin being opened.
