title: "The Shrub’s Errand"

At seven each morning, the town unfolded from a map kept beneath the baker’s counter. Streets shook out their creases; windows blinked flour from their lashes. Only the shrub in the square remained wrinkled. It had been planted in a blue teacup, yet its roots descended through the pavement and, according to the baker, held the map steady when the weather became argumentative.

One brisk Tuesday, the shrub began handing out instructions. Its leaves detached themselves, sailed into people's pockets, and whispered useful commands: Turn your umbrella inside out. Apologize to the third stair. Carry an orange to the post office. Because the shrub had always seemed helpful, everyone obeyed. By noon, inverted umbrellas were catching blue crumbs of sky, the third stair was blushing, and the post office smelled brilliantly of citrus.

Mara, who repaired clocks without telling them, received no leaf. This omission bothered her more than an insult. She approached the teacup and asked what service the town required of her. The shrub rustled, though the air stood still. At last, a small branch pointed toward the edge of the map, where the river had been drawn in pencil and never quite believed in itself.

Mara walked there briskly, carrying the town’s largest eraser. The river was curled beside the final road, refusing to flow because it feared being rubbed away. Along its bank, stranded fish practiced being silver commas. Mara sat down and used the eraser on her own reflection instead. First her hat vanished, then her worried eyebrows, then the expression she wore when expecting instructions.

The river loosened with a sound like a drawer opening. Water rushed past, carrying her erased reflection into fields beyond the paper. Back in the square, every leaf returned to the shrub except one, which settled in Mara’s palm. It was blank.

“Is this all?” she asked.

The shrub bent close, warm with the day’s collected weather. Its berries clicked like tiny abacuses. Mara waited, but the blank leaf remained politely silent. So she wrote nothing on it, and for the first time the town found its own way home.
