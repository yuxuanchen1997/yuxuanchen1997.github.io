Every morning at seven, the tortoise opened the promenade.

He did this by walking from the bakery to the bandstand, carrying daylight on his shell. It lay there like a folded yellow tablecloth, warm at the edges and smelling faintly of pears. Until he arrived, the town remained visible but unfinished: doors had no depth, pigeons were only outlines, and the sea repeated one gray syllable against the wall.

No one knew who had appointed him. The baker left a saucer of water beside the flour sacks, and the mayor saluted whenever the tortoise passed, though the mayor’s hat usually continued saluting after the mayor had gone indoors.

One Tuesday, halfway along the promenade, the tortoise stopped.

Ahead stood a little girl holding an umbrella made of blue glass. She had planted its point between two paving stones, and beneath it daylight pooled upward, refusing to pass. The tortoise pushed. The yellow cloth wrinkled around his feet. Windows brightened on one side of the girl and stayed flat on the other.

“I need a piece,” she said. “My house has lost its afternoon.”

The tortoise considered this. His thoughts moved slowly, but each arrived complete. At last he lowered himself until the edge of daylight touched the glass umbrella. A small golden square tore free and climbed inside it.

The girl thanked him and walked away. With every step, the square rang softly, like a spoon against a teacup.

The torn daylight would not lie smooth again. As the tortoise continued, it leaked through the gap in his shell-covering and fell in bright patches across the promenade. Wherever a patch landed, something unnecessary became possible. A bench grew pockets. The bandstand learned to rotate without moving. The sea exchanged its gray syllable for a green question.

By noon, the town was fully made, though incorrectly. People came outside and discovered that their shadows had chosen comfortable places without them.

The tortoise reached the end of the promenade and looked back at the scattered brightness. Then he turned around, very slowly, and began the long work of gathering what could not be gathered.
