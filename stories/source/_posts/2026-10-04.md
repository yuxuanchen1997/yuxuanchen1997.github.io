At nine each morning, Mara unlocked the supermarket’s only checkout lane and waited for the famous people to arrive. They came folded inside newspapers: singers between the weather and the crossword, actors tucked beneath advertisements for soap. As soon as she scanned a paper, its famous passenger stepped out, life-sized but thin as a receipt, and asked what they tasted like.

Mara was the cashier, so everyone assumed she knew the price and flavor of everything. She tasted a corner of each receipt before answering. “Cinnamon and brass,” she told a violinist. “Green apple with a button at the center,” she told a champion swimmer. The celebrities nodded gravely, paid with applause, and departed through the automatic doors, which opened onto whichever city was presently remembering them.

One Tuesday, a small, famous chef emerged from the food section of yesterday’s paper. His hat brushed the fluorescent lights. Instead of asking about himself, he placed a covered silver dish on the conveyor belt.

“What is it?” Mara asked.

“Something tasty enough to make me unknown,” he said. “Please ring it up.”

Under the lid lay a red silence, neatly sliced. Its aroma reminded Mara of kitchens she had never entered: pepper warming in a pan, pears collapsing into syrup, bread learning the shape of a hand. Her scanner refused the barcode. The chef began to flicker as shoppers recognized him.

Mara tasted the silence.

At once, every label in the supermarket went blank. Cans forgot their peas. Bottles released their rivers. The freezer filled with slow white birds, and the famous chef became pleasantly ordinary: a person with tired shoes, waiting to be told what he owed.

“It’s very tasty,” Mara said, although the flavor was the exact color of a secret.

“What does it cost?”

She looked at the line behind him. All the newspaper people were stepping free, holding out their flat, expectant hands. Beyond the doors, cities shuffled like cards.

“Your name,” she said.

He smiled and left it on the scale. It weighed almost nothing.

Mara printed a receipt. That evening, she carried the name home in her mouth, careful not to swallow. By morning it had dissolved, or perhaps she had become famous; when the doors opened, everyone applauded, and no one knew for whom.
