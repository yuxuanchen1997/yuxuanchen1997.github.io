At the lost-and-found on Aubrey Street, Odile catalogs the things nobody has lost.

A man arrives at opening with a Tuesday folded over his arm like a wet coat. "Found it under the trestle bridge," he says. "It isn't mine." She writes it into the ledger — one Tuesday, mild, intermittent rain, slight fraying at the afternoon — and shelves it in aisle four, between a held breath and the smell of a hardware store in 1988.

The rules are posted by the door in a font nobody reads. Items are held forty years. Proof of ownership required. Management is not responsible.

Nothing is ever claimed. This is the part that troubles her. People bring in the sound of a car pulling into a gravel driveway, an argument still warm from the mouth, a dog's name, the particular blue of a swimming pool at nine in the morning. They set these on the counter and leave quickly, the way people leave hospitals. But no one comes back through the door saying I've been looking everywhere for that.

In the evenings she walks the aisles with the lamp and the ledger and listens to the shelves murmur among themselves.

On a Thursday in the dead middle of the year, she finds a laugh on shelf nine. It is her mother's. She knows it the way you know your own handwriting — the small crack at the end of it, like a stair giving.

Odile carries it to the counter. She stands on the wrong side of the counter, which she has never done, and waits for herself to appear.

Proof of ownership, says the ledger.

She has none. She has a face, a memory, thirty-one years of a house on Aubrey Street. None of these are documents.

She writes it back into the book and returns it to the shelf, gently, so it doesn't wake.
