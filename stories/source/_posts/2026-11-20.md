At the edge of Bellweather stood an elm that refused water. Every dawn its roots pushed empty cups through the soil, porcelain mouths waiting for something sweeter. Mara, who kept the town’s weather in labeled jars, poured rain into one cup. The elm shuddered and grew a single brown leaf, glossy as a polished shoe.

That afternoon a green gecko arrived on the bakery window, carrying a tiny brass key beneath its tongue. It tapped the glass until Mara let it in, then marched straight to the cooling racks. With the key it unlocked a bar of chocolate. Inside was a warm room no larger than a recipe card, furnished with three red chairs and the smell of August.

The gecko entered first. Mara followed, folding herself thin as a bookmark. The third chair remained empty, though it sighed whenever the elm’s branches scratched the bakery roof. On the table lay a map drawn in melted chocolate. Its rivers led nowhere; its roads curled back into the cocoa-colored forest marked ELM, where every tree wore a small white apron.

“The elm is hungry for directions,” said the gecko, speaking in Mara’s own voice. “If we do not feed it, it will root the town to one place.” Already outside, streets were tightening like drawstrings. The school leaned against the fountain; the post office slid beneath the bakery’s awning. People opened doors and found the same square waiting on both sides.

Mara broke the map into pieces and carried them out in her pockets. At each crossing she buried a chocolate fragment. The gecko darted ahead, turning its tail like a compass needle. Wherever the chocolate softened underground, the elm’s roots tasted a possible road: the market toward Tuesday, the bridge toward blue wind, the narrow lane toward a question nobody had yet asked.

By evening the town loosened. Houses remembered their distances, and the elm drank uncertainty through every root. Its brown leaf divided into a hundred chocolate leaves, each stamped with a different doorway. The gecko locked the last one with its brass key and vanished behind the bark.

Mara kept the empty red chair on her windowsill. Some mornings it sighed. Then she would unwrap one leaf, taste the road printed on it, and walk until the town became a place it had never been before.
