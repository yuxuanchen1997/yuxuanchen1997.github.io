title: "The Question-Key"

At nine each morning, the city of Folded Umbrellas hired an illustrator to repair its weather. Rain had begun falling upward, carrying puddles into the rafters and leaving the streets dry. The jolly mayor, whose laughter shook buttons from coats, gave him a brush made of celery and pointed toward the municipal panda. “Draw us a sensible afternoon,” she said, “or everything will float away thirsty.”

The panda was named Porcelain, though he was soft and smelled faintly of toast. He sat in the central square, holding a blank parasol, while shopkeepers tied teacups to their wrists. The illustrator opened his portfolio. Every page showed the same cheerful yellow door, with no wall around it. Whenever he tried to draw clouds, the door appeared instead, grinning through its brass knocker.

“Perhaps it wants to be opened,” Porcelain said. His voice sounded like a broom sweeping sugar. The illustrator confessed that he had never drawn a key. At this, the mayor’s jolliness escaped her mouth as three red balloons. They bounced among the rising raindrops and began erasing the names from street signs. Soon no one knew where the bakery ended or the school began.

Porcelain dipped one black paw into the upward rain. On the parasol he painted a key shaped like a question. The illustrator copied it, but his celery brush sprouted leaves, then roots, then a tiny staircase descending into the paper. Together they climbed down. Beneath the page lay a room crowded with unfinished things: half a violin, the left half of Tuesday, and a river waiting for its verb.

The yellow door stood at the far end, humming. Porcelain fitted the question-key into its lock. Instead of opening, the door folded itself into a bright paper boat. The illustrator drew an oar; the panda drew a second. They rowed the boat up the staircase and into the square, where it sailed among the raindrops, gathering every puddle and pouring it gently back onto the stones.

By afternoon, the streets shone. The balloons returned the stolen names, though not always correctly: the bakery became “Tomorrow,” and the school became “Open Carefully.” The mayor laughed with less force. Porcelain kept the parasol, now covered in small doors. The illustrator never learned to draw sensible weather, but from then on, whenever the city misplaced an afternoon, he and the panda illustrated another way home.
