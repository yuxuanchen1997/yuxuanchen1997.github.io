Every Tuesday, the town of Bellweather boiled its streets. At dawn, municipal cooks rolled the avenues into kettles, salted the intersections, and stirred until the cobblestones softened into ribbons of pasta. Citizens traveled by twirling sidewalks around silver forks. By noon, the town was usually al dente.

Mara repaired the chain that kept Tuesday attached to Wednesday. It ran beneath the clock tower, each link warm and flour-dusted, tightening whenever anyone hurried. Her only tool was a walnut inherited from her aunt, its shell engraved with two tiny doors. When pressed to a broken link, it whispered which side had forgotten the other.

One Tuesday, the streets refused to be eaten. They climbed from the kettles in glistening ropes, looped themselves through windows, and tied every fork to the sky. Beneath the clock tower, Mara found the chain sagging into a bowl. Wednesday had vanished from its far end.

“Which side forgot?” she asked the walnut.

Both doors opened. From one emerged the smell of rain on laundry; from the other, a small procession of ants carrying blank calendars. The walnut said, “Wednesday is hiding inside what Tuesday has not finished.”

Mara followed the rebellious pasta to the town square. There, every noodle had braided itself into a single golden cable. It tugged at the clock tower, yearning not upward but sideways, toward a narrow place between one minute and the next. Mara cracked the walnut—not with force, but by telling it a secret she had never known: “I am tired of repairing time for people who chew without tasting.”

Inside lay no kernel. There was a room no larger than a thimble, where Wednesday sat barefoot, washing sauce from its hands. Mara offered it the loose end of the chain. Wednesday shook its head.

“Only if Tuesday comes too.”

So Mara fastened the golden pasta to the final link. The chain shuddered; clocks spilled twelve bright numbers into the square. Tuesday and Wednesday moved forward together, awkwardly, like friends carrying a long table through a narrow door.

Afterward, Bellweather stopped boiling all its streets. One avenue remained pasta, winding warm through the town. People walked beside it slowly, breaking off pieces only when hungry. Mara kept the cracked walnut on her desk. Through its tiny doors came the sound of dishes being set for a day that had not yet decided its name.
