Every Tuesday, the tutor arrived by unfolding from the vine outside Elian’s kitchen window. First came a polished shoe, then a sleeve full of chalk, then the rest of him, narrow and green-eyed, smelling faintly of rainwater and pepper.

He taught subjects absent from ordinary schools: the arithmetic of echoes, the grammar of steam, the proper way to apologize to a locked door. Elian struggled with all of them. Whenever he made a mistake, a small blue mushroom appeared somewhere in the house.

At first, the mushrooms were harmless. One balanced on the sugar bowl. Another opened beneath the piano pedal and hummed whenever anyone crossed the room. But by midsummer, hundreds crowded the cupboards, floated in the bath, and gathered under chairs like dropped punctuation. Elian’s parents began eating supper standing up.

“You must improve,” said the tutor, sharpening a pencil against his wrist. “Your errors require soil.”

Elian studied until numbers crawled from the pages and nested in his hair. Still, the mushrooms multiplied. Finally, in frustration, he asked the locked pantry door why it refused to open.

“Because nobody has knocked from the inside,” the door replied.

This seemed less like an answer than a dare. Elian pressed his ear to the wood. Behind it, something tapped three times.

The tutor turned pale. The vine around his ankle tightened.

Elian apologized to the door—not for wanting passage, but for assuming it had only one side. The latch lifted. Inside the pantry stood a bright field beneath a yellow sky, and across it stretched the same vine, immense as a road. Mushrooms lined its edges, each wearing a tiny blue cap and whispering one of Elian’s mistakes.

The tutor stepped through and began gathering them tenderly into his sleeves.

“They were never yours,” Elian said.

“No,” the tutor admitted. “I was taught that mistakes belong to the student. It is an old lesson, and a poor one.”

Together they replanted every mushroom in the field. By evening, each error had opened into a little umbrella of shade. The vine loosened its grip, curled around the tutor’s hand, and led him away.

After that, Elian kept the pantry door unlocked. On Tuesdays, he sat in the blue shade and tutored it in the difficult art of being a window.
