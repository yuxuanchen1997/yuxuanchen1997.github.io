In the town of Bellwether, every household kept a small brass weather in a drawer. On clear mornings it was warm and smooth; before rain, tiny clouds wandered beneath its surface. Only Mara’s weather had stopped changing. It showed a permanent afternoon, yellow and windless, with one black bird nailed to the sky.

Mara carried it to Iven, the town’s finest craftsperson and its last licensed engraver. His workshop occupied the hollow inside an enormous wooden pear. Drawers opened in the walls like sleepy mouths, and silver tools hung from the ceiling by threads of steam. Iven examined the brass disc through a lens filled with blue water.

“Someone has engraved certainty into it,” he said. “Certainty is harder than metal.”

He set the weather beneath his needle. Instead of cutting a line, the needle drew out a long golden filament. It smelled of bread crust and distant applause. As Iven pulled, the fixed afternoon loosened. The black bird flapped once. From the widening groove spilled a sparkle—not a flash, but a small, nimble creature with six glass legs. It raced across the bench, polishing everything it touched into unbearable perfection.

The sparkle polished Iven’s hammer until it could strike only flawless blows. It polished Mara’s reflection until she no longer recognized her own patient face. Then it leapt from the workshop and skittered through Bellwether. Crooked doors straightened. Soup arranged itself into symmetrical circles. Children’s untidy drawings became accurate maps of places nobody wished to visit.

Mara understood what the engraver did not. She seized his needle and scratched a wavering line across her brass weather. The mark was clumsy, deep in one place and faint in another. At once, rain began inside the disc. The sparkle returned, furious, slipping on the imperfect groove. Iven caught it beneath an overturned teacup.

Together, craftsperson and customer spent the afternoon engraving mistakes into the town: a stutter in every staircase, a thumbprint in each window, one sour note inside every bell. By evening, Bellwether had become recognizable again.

Mara’s weather never worked properly. Some days it snowed while the streets steamed. Sometimes the brass bird swam. But whenever the trapped sparkle rattled its teacup, her weather produced a small, useful breeze, and nothing remained certain for long.
