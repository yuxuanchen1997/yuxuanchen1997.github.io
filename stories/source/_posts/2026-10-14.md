Every Tuesday, the town of Bellweather woke inside a lime. At dawn, green light seeped through windows, the streets tasted of rind, and every door swelled shut until someone spoke a sour truth. Most citizens kept a confession ready. The baker admitted he disliked bread; the schoolmistress confessed that seven seemed unnecessary. Their doors sighed open, and business began.

Mara, the messenger, worked in a greenhouse of juniper shrubs. Letters did not arrive there; they ripened as blue berries, each holding an address beneath its skin. That Tuesday, every berry bore Mara’s name. She crushed one. A small paper staircase unfolded and climbed her sleeve. On its highest step stood three words: PEPPER THE CLOCK. Similar staircases rustled from every shrub, crowding toward her wrist.

Bellweather’s clock occupied the market square and had no hands, only a mouth of polished brass. Mara borrowed the baker’s pepper mill and ground until the mouth sneezed. Noon flew out: a white bird made of twelve folded napkins. Without noon, shadows loosened from their owners and queued at the fountain, murmuring that the lime was tightening. Already the houses leaned together, squeezed by the thickening green world.

Mara chased the paper bird back to the greenhouse. It pecked a juniper berry, which opened like an eye. Inside was not an address but a tiny map showing Bellweather as a seed lodged in the lime’s center. Around it, in Mara’s handwriting, ran a question: WHERE DO YOU BELONG WHEN NO ONE IS DELIVERING YOU? She had no prepared truth. The lime contracted; roofs chimed against one another. Pepper stung the air. Juniper roots lifted, waiting like green fingers.

“I belong where messages change the messenger,” Mara said, though she did not know whether it was true. The map drank her words. At once, the junipers bent into doorways, and the citizens stepped through them carrying their houses as bright parcels. Mara remained until the last doorstep vanished. Then she peeled a narrow opening in the sky and posted the town into an unknown afternoon.

On Wednesday, Bellweather woke beneath ordinary sunlight. Everyone’s tongue tasted of pepper; every pocket contained a juniper berry. In the square, Mara’s empty satchel hung from the clock’s mouth. It smelled sharply of lime, and when the wind entered it, the whole town heard an address being whispered—almost theirs, but not quite.
