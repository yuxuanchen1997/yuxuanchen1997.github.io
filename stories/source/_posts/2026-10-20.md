title: "The Grain of Noon"
date: 2026-10-20 00:00:00

Every morning, Mara unlocked the greenhouse and found the wooden floor rearranged into a new map. Planks that had pointed toward the door now curved around the lemon trees; knots gathered into islands; nailheads marked cities no atlas admitted. At the center sat a lemur with sawdust on its black hands, studying the geography it had made.

The greenhouse belonged to no one. It had appeared between the bakery and the river one Tuesday, warm with rain that never reached the street. Mara had entered because a small sign read: CARETAKER WANTED. MUST UNDERSTAND WOOD. She did not, but the door accepted her answer.

At noon, the lemur began knocking on the floor. The boards answered from below: oak in a deep voice, pine in a quick one, cedar with the patience of cupboards. Mara pressed her ear down. They were arguing about where the greenhouse should travel next. Oak wanted the mountains. Pine wanted the railway. Cedar wanted to remain and grow old around the weather.

“You built the map,” Mara told the lemur. “You decide.”

The lemur shook its striped tail and handed her a green fig. Inside, instead of seeds, were tiny rooms: a kitchen boiling blue soup, a classroom where chairs took notes, a bedroom folding itself into an envelope. In each room stood Mara, older or younger, listening for a knock beneath the floor.

Then the river rose without becoming higher. It simply leaned sideways, pressing its silver weight against the glass. The plants lifted their pots like skirts. The wooden map loosened, each plank eager for its chosen direction. Mara understood that if the boards departed separately, the greenhouse would become only weather.

She bit the fig. All her other selves looked up.

“Here,” they said together.

Mara removed her shoes and stood beside the lemur. With her heel she knocked a rhythm belonging to no destination. The wood fell silent. Then every plank turned one degree, not toward mountain, railway, or age, but toward the room in the fig where no Mara had yet appeared.

The river straightened. The pots settled. Beyond the glass, the bakery and street were gone. A white door waited among the tomatoes. The lemur opened it, and warm sawdust blew in from somewhere that had not been built.
