Every morning, Engineer Lin unfolded the city from a gray handkerchief.

First came the bridges, damp and flexible as noodles. Then the apartment towers rose, shaking crumbs from their windows. Last came the citizens, who stepped from the cloth already dressed for work, carrying briefcases full of weather. By eight, rain had been assigned to the accountants, sunlight to the bakers, and a small, persistent wind to the schoolchildren.

Lin’s only assistant was an ant named Minister. Minister inspected every hinge in the city and marked unsafe structures with a grain of sugar. He had never marked anything until the morning he climbed onto Lin’s thumbnail and drew a white circle there.

“What is failing?” Lin asked.

Minister pointed beneath the handkerchief.

Lin had never considered beneath. The city stood upon the cloth; the cloth stood upon the table; the table, according to professional habit, required no explanation. Yet when Lin lifted one gray corner, a warm draft rose, smelling of pencils and pears. Far below hung another city, upside down, suspended from the stitches. Its citizens walked along the undersides of roads. Their clouds were square. Their engineer, an enormous ant in silver spectacles, was unfolding Lin’s workshop from a handkerchief the color of thunder.

At that moment, both engineers looked up.

The enormous ant raised one feeler. Minister raised his. Between them, the cloth tightened. Buildings in both cities leaned toward the stitches, and all the assigned weather escaped from the briefcases. Rain fell upward. Sunlight pooled in stairwells. The schoolchildren’s wind circled the table, searching for a classroom.

Lin understood that the handkerchief was not a foundation but a treaty, and that one careless repair could pull either republic through the other. With a needle no longer than Minister’s shadow, Lin stitched the white circle on his thumbnail to its corresponding circle below.

The two cities steadied. The enormous ant bowed and folded away the workshop.

Since then, Lin leaves one corner of the gray handkerchief slightly raised. Through the narrow gap, citizens exchange impossible goods: square clouds for round windows, pear-scented pencils for jars of upward rain. Minister collects the customs duty, one grain at a time, and the schoolchildren’s wind has finally found its classroom—beneath the table, where it teaches maps to chairs.
