In Bellweather, fog did not drift. It arrived at dawn as a solid white wall, fitting itself between chimneys and sealing every street from roof to cobblestone. Doors opened onto its cool, unyielding surface. Sparrows stood upon it as if on plaster, pecking for crumbs of yesterday. By breakfast, the town had forgotten what lay across the road.

Mara kept the municipal brass instruments in a narrow workshop beneath the bakery: horns, compasses, hinges, and one immense tuning fork taller than she was. Each object had been polished until it held a small, curved version of the room. Her duty was to test the fog. She struck the fork with a felt hammer, and its note bored a round tunnel through the whiteness. Bakers rolled loaves through it to customers. Children crawled to school on their knees, singing so the passage would not close.

One Tuesday, the fork made no sound. Instead, it bent like warm grass and pointed toward Mara's pocket. There she found a brass key she had never seen, heavy as a promise. When she pressed it into the fog, a keyhole appeared. She turned it. The entire wall clicked.

A room opened where the avenue should have been. It contained no furniture, only hundreds of glass jars, each labeled with an hour the town had misplaced: “The Afternoon Everyone Overslept,” “Seven Minutes of Unasked Questions,” “The Long Lunch of Falling Spoons.” Inside, time fluttered like colored paper.

Mara chose a jar marked “Tomorrow, Before It Hardens.” The lid resisted, then gave with a sigh. A wind rushed out, smelling of wet stone and oranges. It poured through the keyhole and softened the streets. The solid fog slumped into ordinary weather. Houses reappeared, surprised to find themselves opposite one another. People waved across distances they had considered imaginary.

By noon, the brass instruments had tarnished green, except for the key. Mara hung it above the workshop door. Sometimes, when the air thickened, it turned by itself, unlocking a cupboard, a window, or the silent space between two people. No one agreed what came through. But afterward, something that had seemed solid always learned how to move.
