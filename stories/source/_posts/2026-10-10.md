At the edge of Bellweather Square stood a trusty green bench that everyone called Trusty. It never splintered, never wobbled, and always had room for one more person, even when twenty umbrellas already occupied it. Each dawn, the baker Mara sat there with a loaf still warm beneath her coat and asked the bench what the day would cost.

“Something fresh,” Trusty usually replied.

Mara paid with small things: the scent of cut pears, the shine on a newly washed cup, the first joke told by a child. The bench tucked each freshness between its slats. In return, it warned her about falling chimneys, dishonest peaches, and hours that intended to arrive twice.

One Tuesday, Trusty demanded the freshest thing in the city.

Mara searched the market. The fish had rehearsed their silver overnight. Mint leaves wore green uniforms. A fountain produced water with yesterday’s fingerprints. Nothing qualified. By noon, shopkeepers had begun wrapping their goods in old newspapers, ashamed. Even the wind smelled borrowed.

Then Mara noticed a narrow door painted on Trusty’s backrest. It opened into a bright waiting room where hundreds of unused mornings sat in glass jars. Some contained rain rising from streets; others held unspoken greetings, clean aprons, or roads with no footprints. A clerk made of folded receipts explained that Trusty had guarded the city’s freshness for years. Now the jars were nearly full, and unless one was opened, tomorrow would have nowhere to begin.

“Which one?” Mara asked.

“The one you trust,” said the clerk.

Mara chose a jar with no label. When she lifted its lid, a blue smell escaped—not the smell of anything blue, but the smell blue had before it became a color. It swept through the square. Bread forgot its recipes and rose into soft, edible clouds. The fountain invented a new direction. Every window opened onto a different kitchen.

Trusty sighed, suddenly light. Its green paint peeled away in ribbons and became grass. Beneath Mara, the bench was now plain wood, slightly crooked, with room for exactly two.

She sat. After a moment, the clerk sat beside her, no longer folded.

“What will the day cost?” Mara asked.

“Nothing,” the former clerk said. “It is fresh because nobody has priced it yet.”
