At dawn, the surveyor arrived with a brass tripod and discovered that the island had moved seven inches during the night. It had not drifted east or west. It had risen, delicately, like a knee beneath a tablecloth.

The only other inhabitant, an upholsterer named Pell, accepted this news without surprise. For years Pell had been covering the island’s stones in blue velvet. He claimed the stones complained of hardness after sunset. Each morning he listened to them, chose the unhappiest, and fitted it with padding, fringe, and small pearl buttons. By now the shore resembled a waiting room where the chairs had forgotten how to stand.

The surveyor drove a red stake into the highest hill. The hill sighed, and the stake vanished to its ribbon. When she bent to inspect the hole, she heard rigging creak below. Pell brought scissors. Together they cut a careful square in the grass and peeled it back.

Under the island sailed a white sailboat.

It moved through compact yellow light, its mast brushing roots, its wake full of teaspoons. At the tiller sat no one. Instead, a measuring tape wound around the wheel, tightening whenever the island rose. The surveyor understood: every line she had drawn on her maps was a rope, and every boundary had pulled the hidden boat closer to the surface.

“We must give it room,” Pell said.

They spent the day unmaking certainty. The surveyor erased the coastline from her charts. Pell stripped velvet from the stones and stitched the pieces into a vast patchwork sail. As the last border disappeared, the island opened along its center seam. The sailboat rose through fields and kitchens, carrying the two of them without spilling a cup.

By evening, they were sailing across an ocean the color of unfinished wood. Behind them, the island folded itself into a small upholstered suitcase. Ahead, distances wandered freely, refusing to be counted. The surveyor threw her ruler overboard. Pell saved one pearl button in his pocket.

They never agreed on where they went. On certain maps, their route appears as a loose blue thread. On others, it is the narrow space between one number and the next, padded so softly that no traveler feels the crossing.
