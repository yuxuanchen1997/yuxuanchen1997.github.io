title: "The Table That Remembered Water"

Every morning, the lagoon rearranged its islets before the fishermen woke. Most drifted only a little, exchanging palms or flocks of white birds, but the smallest islet traveled furthest. It carried nothing except a blue wooden table, set for four.

Mara had watched it from her bakery window since childhood. At sunrise, the table stood near the eastern reeds; by noon, it might be circling the bell tower’s reflection. No one visited it. No one admitted knowing who kept the plates polished or why warm bread appeared beneath four folded napkins.

One windless Tuesday, the islet bumped gently against Mara’s doorstep.

She stepped onto it with a basket of rolls. The lagoon tightened around the shore like a belt, and the village became strangely flat, every house no thicker than a playing card. At the table, three chairs pulled themselves back. The fourth remained still.

Mara sat there.

Water rose inside the empty glasses. In one she saw herself at seven, releasing paper boats into a gutter. In another, she was forty, teaching loaves to float. The third contained a room she had never entered, where rain fell upward from the floor and collected on the ceiling.

Then the table spoke—not in words, but by opening a narrow drawer in its side. Inside lay thousands of crumbs, each humming faintly. Mara recognized the melodies: every conversation abandoned too soon in the village, every apology swallowed, every question saved for later.

The other chairs filled with shapes made of lagoon water. They leaned toward her, waiting.

Mara placed her fresh rolls on the plates. “I don’t know what you want,” she said.

At once, the watery guests began to eat. With every bite, a window in the distant village opened. Voices crossed the lagoon: neighbors calling forgotten names, children finishing interrupted stories, the bell keeper confessing that the bell rang itself.

By sunset, the islet returned Mara to her bakery. The blue table sailed away, lighter now, its drawer silent.

The next morning, the lagoon left every islet exactly where it was. Yet in the village, all the tables had moved during the night. They stood beside open doors, set with an extra plate.
