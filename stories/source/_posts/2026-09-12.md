---
title: "The Orchard of Unfinished Songs"
date: 2026-09-12 00:00:00

Every morning, the town woke inside a different sound. Tuesday was a cupboard slamming underwater. Wednesday was the patient hiss of blue paint drying. On Thursday, the noise resembled a thousand spoons arguing in a distant kitchen, and no one could hear the church clock, the traffic, or their own apologies.

Only Mara, the town’s last musician, could walk through the uproar without covering her ears. She carried an olive in the hollow of her violin, where a heart might have rested if instruments were permitted hearts. The olive had been given to her by a tree that grew in the railway station and produced fruit only when a train decided not to arrive.

Mara believed the town’s noises were unfinished songs. She spent her days catching them in glass jars: the bark of a yellow dog, the click of a woman folding a map, the thunderous whisper of bread rising. At night she opened the jars above her violin. The olive trembled, the strings brightened, and the sounds arranged themselves into small staircases that climbed the walls and vanished through the ceiling.

Then came Saturday, noisier than all the other days. The streets roared like pockets full of machinery. Windows shouted their own addresses. Teacups rang whenever someone remembered a promise. Beneath everything pulsed one stubborn note, so low that buildings bent toward it like flowers.

Mara followed the note to the orchard beyond the station. Every tree there bore olives shaped like closed ears. In the center stood an empty chair, conducting the branches with its wooden arms. Mara placed her violin beneath her chin and played the single note the town had forgotten: not silence, but the sound just before silence chooses what to become.

The olive inside the violin split open. From it flew a small green door. The door opened in midair, and Saturday’s noise rushed through, taking the staircases, the shouting windows, and the quarrelsome spoons with it. When the door snapped shut, the town heard leaves touching one another for the first time.

Mara planted the broken olive beside the empty chair. By morning it had grown into a tree covered with tiny violins. None had strings. Still, whenever the wind passed through them, the townspeople stopped speaking, certain they had almost remembered the words.
