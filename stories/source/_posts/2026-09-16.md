title: "The Doorway’s Rings"

Every morning, the town of Bellweather polished its brass doorway. It stood alone in the square, without wall or roof, its frame bolted into a circle of white stones. When the last fingerprint vanished from its surface, the doorway opened—not inward or outward, but upward—and released the smell of rain falling somewhere else.

Mara, apprentice to the municipal polisher, had been warned never to step beneath it. Her task was to rub the brass until she could see a future version of her face. Usually the reflection showed her older by a minute. On the first Tuesday of summer, however, it showed no face at all. Instead, a sequoia filled the metal, its red trunk rising through clouds of blue flour.

Before Mara could call her supervisor, a cone dropped from the reflection and landed at her feet. It was warm and ticking. She planted it between two paving stones. By noon, a slender sequoia had grown through the square, lifting benches in its branches and balancing the fountain like a glass bowl. The townspeople climbed after their belongings, complaining about sap on official documents.

The doorway, meanwhile, began shrinking.

Mara understood without knowing how. The tree had come through too early; something must go back before the brass frame closed. Yet nobody wished to surrender a bench, a fountain, or even a sap-stained permit. Mara placed her polishing cloth on the lowest root. The sequoia folded around it like a hand accepting a coin, but the doorway continued to narrow, singing one clear note.

Then Mara stepped beneath the lintel.

She did not disappear. The square did. For one breath she stood in a forest where every sequoia wore a brass doorway halfway up its trunk. Tiny towns moved behind their shining surfaces, polishing themselves feverishly. Mara touched the nearest tree. Its rings loosened, spun like nested wheels, and one wooden circle rolled into her palm.

When Bellweather returned, the solitary doorway was gone. In its place stood the young sequoia, no taller than Mara. Set into its trunk was a brass ring exactly large enough for one eye. Each morning, the townspeople queued to look through it. They saw rain, blue flour, or their faces one minute younger. Mara alone saw a doorway, growing patiently around the town.
