title: "The Menu of Unlived Days"

Every morning, the printer in Ada’s kitchen produced a photograph instead of a page. The photographs arrived warm, smelling faintly of toast, and showed meals she had never cooked: a blue soup holding six tiny umbrellas, a loaf sliced into windows, pears sleeping beneath transparent blankets.

Ada pasted each photograph into an album labeled UNLIVED DAYS. Beneath every image, she wrote the date and left a space for the recipe. By spring, the album had grown too heavy for its shelf. It began opening by itself at noon, turning its pages with the patient rhythm of someone breathing.

One Tuesday, the printer delivered a picture of Ada standing at her stove. In the photograph, she was stirring an empty pot while a second Ada watched from inside the album. Between them sat a plate bearing a single red button.

Ada decided to cook the picture exactly. She placed a button on her best plate, set an empty pot over a low flame, and stirred. At once, the kitchen lengthened like warm sugar. Cupboards retreated toward a distant horizon. The table sprouted four extra corners. From the printer came the sound of silverware arguing politely.

Then the album climbed down from its shelf.

It opened to a blank page and waited. Ada understood—not clearly, but with the certainty one gives to salt—that the meal required a memory. She chose an afternoon from childhood: rain stippling the pavement, her father teaching her to whistle through a blade of grass. She spoke it over the pot. The memory softened, folded, and became steam.

The printer shuddered. Out slid a new photograph: the kitchen restored, the album closed, the button sewn onto Ada’s yellow coat. But outside the window, every passerby carried a steaming bowl, though no two bowls contained the same weather.

Ada turned off the flame. The empty pot was full of whistling.

She did not paste the final photograph into the album. Instead, she set it on the table and ate its white border first. It tasted of flour, rain, and a road she had not yet taken. When she reached the picture itself, the tiny Ada inside looked up, lifted her spoon, and waited for the cook to choose what happened next.
