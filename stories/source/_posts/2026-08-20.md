---
title: "The Rooms Above the Terrace"
date: 2026-08-20 00:00:00
lang: en
layout: post
---

Every morning, the receptionist polished the brass bell until it reflected a room that did not exist. In the reflection, hydrangeas crowded a blue terrace, nodding beneath a wind that never touched the hotel lobby. Guests sometimes leaned close and asked what floor the terrace was on. The receptionist always answered, “The floor above your question.”

One Tuesday, a hydrangea petal slipped out of the bell and landed on the register. Wherever its blue edge touched, the written names rearranged themselves into instructions: OPEN THE WINDOW IN ROOM ZERO. WATER THE CHAIR. DO NOT LET THE TERRACE REMEMBER YOU.

The hotel had no Room Zero, but the receptionist found its key inside a bowl of sugar cubes. The key was warm and smelled of rain. It opened the elevator, though the elevator had never possessed a keyhole. Instead of rising, the cabin widened, becoming a corridor lined with doors that breathed in and out.

At the corridor’s end waited the terrace from the bell. Hundreds of hydrangeas grew from teacups, shoes, and overturned clocks. Their blossoms changed color whenever the receptionist blinked. In the center stood a wooden chair, its roots gripping the tiles.

The receptionist filled a silver watering can from a fountain that poured upward. When the water touched the chair, it unfolded like a patient insect. Beneath its seat lay all the sentences guests had swallowed before checking in: apologies, recipes, small rebellions, the names of forgotten streets. They fluttered around the terrace, seeking mouths.

Then the hydrangeas began whispering the receptionist’s own name. The warning returned: DO NOT LET THE TERRACE REMEMBER YOU. Quickly, the receptionist rang the brass bell carried in an apron pocket. The sound became a round yellow door. Before stepping through, the receptionist plucked one white hydrangea and placed it on the rooted chair.

Back in the lobby, the register was blank. Guests arrived with suitcases full of ordinary weather. None remembered the terrace. But each morning, beside the polished bell, a white hydrangea waited in a glass, and the receptionist wore a name tag bearing a different name—each one belonging to a street that had never been built.
