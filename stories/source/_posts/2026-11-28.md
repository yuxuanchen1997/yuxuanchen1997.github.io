Every morning, Mara unlocked the Indoor Weather Office, though it had no doors leading outside. Its corridors were arranged inside a retired wardrobe, and each drawer contained a climate approved for domestic use: drizzle for ferns, a thumb-sized wind for cooling soup, six inches of sunshine to lay across damp socks. Citizens arrived carrying jars and prescriptions.

Mara’s job was to decide whether each request was useful. She stamped USEFUL in blue ink on breezes that turned ceiling fans, fog that softened stale bread, and small thunderstorms prescribed for people who could not begin a conversation. Useless weather was returned to its drawer, where it muttered against the brass handles.

One Tuesday, a child named Ivo placed an empty lunchbox on her desk. “I need something bold,” he said, “bold enough to make our walls admit they are movable.”

Mara consulted the manual. Bold weather was listed between Kitchen Hail and Bedroom Tide, but its page had been removed. Behind the index she found a warning written in red: BOLDNESS MAY CAUSE ROOMS TO REMEMBER OTHER SHAPES. She looked through the office window. Across the city, families lived indoors within indoors: houses nested in warehouses, warehouses tucked inside painted cupboards, cupboards stored in the pockets of larger houses.

“That does not sound useful,” Mara said.

Ivo opened the lunchbox. From it came the smell of an orchard no building inspector had ever measured. Mara’s stamp trembled. Then she led him to the final drawer, which required both of them to pull. Inside waited a square of weather striped yellow and green, as solid as folded cloth. It leaped into the lunchbox and snapped the lid shut itself.

By afternoon, walls throughout the city had begun changing their minds. Kitchens widened into meadows; elevators developed corners where people could sit beside unexpected fountains. Mara’s office unfolded from the wardrobe and became a long red bridge beneath an indoor sky. None of it was useful in the approved sense.

Still, citizens crossed the bridge carrying chairs, kettles, and sleeping plants. Mara stood at its center, stamping the air in bold blue letters. Each mark opened briefly like a window. Whether those windows led outside, or merely into a larger room, no one could agree—and for once, no one filed a complaint.
