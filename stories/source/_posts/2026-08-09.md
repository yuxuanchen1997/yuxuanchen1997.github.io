title: "The Color Between Stations"

Every morning, Ivo drew a white line from his doorstep to the railway station. By noon, the line had usually wandered elsewhere—through a bakery window, around a sleeping fountain, once straight up the mayor’s blue coat. Nobody knew who moved it. The town simply stepped over it and continued becoming Tuesday.

One damp afternoon, Ivo followed the line beyond the final platform. It entered a field where hundreds of mushrooms stood in orderly rows, their caps tilted like attentive ears. At the center sat a woman stirring a copper vat with a wooden oar. The liquid inside was neither red nor yellow, but the color of a word forgotten just before speaking.

“I make dye for unfinished things,” she said. “Clouds without weather. Doors without rooms. Promises without mouths.”

The white line climbed the vat and vanished beneath the surface. At once, every mushroom sighed. Their pale stems darkened, drawing color from the soil: apricot, ink, green glass, the bruised violet of distant bells. Ivo watched the colors travel underground in delicate threads. Wherever two threads crossed, a small railway station appeared, complete with benches, clocks, and signs naming places that did not exist.

The woman handed him a mushroom no larger than a thimble. “Choose a station.”

Ivo bit the cap. His tongue turned turquoise. Across the field, a platform unfolded beside a sign reading WHERE YOU ALMOST WENT. A train arrived without sound, its carriages painted in changing shades. Through one window he saw himself as a baker kneading maps into bread; through another, he was teaching umbrellas to close during rain. Each life looked toward him briefly, then returned to its work.

Ivo did not board. Instead, he dipped both hands into the vat and lifted the white line out, dripping with impossible color. He carried it home while the stations folded behind him like paper instruments.

The next morning, the town found a bright line running from every doorstep to every other. People followed it cautiously, meeting in kitchens, workshops, and gardens they had passed for years. By noon, Tuesday had become a color nobody could name, though everyone wore it on their hands.
