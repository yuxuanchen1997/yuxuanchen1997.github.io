Every morning, the manager unlocked the Office of Unfinished Destinations and found a camel beside the filing cabinets. Its humps were covered in green felt, like hills borrowed from a billiard table. No one remembered hiring it, but its brass badge read ASSISTANT, and the manager, trusting badges more than memory, assigned it a desk.

The office employed one writer, whose task was to compose places for citizens who had misplaced their plans. She wrote villages inside teacups, staircases leading into afternoons, and a station where trains arrived before anyone decided to travel. The manager stamped each page APPROVED and fed it through a slot in the wall. Beyond the plaster, streets presumably unfolded.

The camel disliked the procedure. Whenever the writer finished a destination, it chewed one corner of the paper and stored the missing words in its mouth. By Thursday it had accumulated harbor, cinnamon, balcony, and several useful verbs. Its breath smelled of maps after rain.

“This is sabotage,” said the manager, who had begun measuring anxiety with paper clips.

“This is editing,” said the writer.

The camel blinked and spat the word elsewhere into the wastebasket.

At once the basket became a blue door. Wind entered with feathers and warm dust from a road that had never agreed to be straight. The filing cabinets trotted through, followed by the desks and the ceiling fan, spinning at knee height. The manager seized the stamp. The writer seized her empty page. The camel lowered itself like a patient bridge.

They crossed together. On the other side stood an endless office made of sand. Each grain bore a tiny sentence, and each sentence requested approval. The manager raised the stamp, but there was no desk beneath his hand. The writer listened to the grains whispering possible towns. The camel opened its mouth and released the stolen words.

They rose as bright awnings above the sand. Harbor leaned against balcony. Cinnamon became a road; useful verbs began pulling carts. The manager watched his stamp soften into a red flower and, for the first time, approved nothing.

By evening, travelers were arriving from directions the writer had not invented. The camel accepted their luggage. The former manager watered the flower. Above the new town, the green felt hills rolled slowly from one horizon to another, searching for a table large enough to hold them.
