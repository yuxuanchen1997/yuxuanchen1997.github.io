On the first overcast morning of summer, Mara found a red balloon tied to the brass handle of her bakery. It was swollen with rain instead of air. Whenever she touched it, a small storm trembled inside, and the loaves cooling behind the window leaned toward it like fields of wheat.

A paper tag hung from the string: PLEASE RETURN THE SKY BEFORE NOON.

Mara closed the bakery and followed the balloon. It pulled her through streets where everyone carried open umbrellas beneath dry clouds. At the fountain, fish swam above the water while their shadows remained below. In the square, a man sold squares of dappled sunlight from a velvet-lined suitcase. Customers pinned them to their coats, and bright leaf-shaped patches wandered over their sleeves.

“Your balloon is leaking,” the seller said.

Silver drops were indeed rising from it. Wherever they touched the clouds, blue holes appeared. Through each hole Mara saw a different room: a kitchen full of singing spoons, a classroom where maps refused to lie flat, her own bakery with no door.

The balloon tugged her toward the clock tower. At the top, she found the sky folded on a table, overcast side outward, like a vast gray tablecloth. Beneath it, something golden kicked impatiently.

Mara untied the balloon. The rain rushed out, washing the gray cloth transparent. The trapped sunlight scattered across the rooftops in dappled pieces, making the entire town resemble an animal waking beneath leaves. The balloon, now empty, whispered, “Choose.”

She could spread the sky smooth and restore the ordinary day, or leave one wrinkle.

Mara pressed her thumb into the cloth.

Afterward, the clouds behaved, the fish returned to the fountain, and the sunlight seller shut his suitcase. But above Mara’s bakery, one permanent crease remained. Every afternoon it opened briefly, releasing a red balloon filled with rain. Mara tied each one to a chair beside the window, where customers drank tea and listened to the small weather breathing.

No one asked where the chairs went at night.
