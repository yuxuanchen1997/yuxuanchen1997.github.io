On the morning the lighthouse arrived, it stood in the town square as if it had always been there, white tower wedged between the bakery and the fountain. No coast lay within three hundred miles. Nevertheless, its lamp revolved, washing the streets with a warm yellow beam. Wherever the light passed, finished things became raw: roof tiles softened into red clay, violins loosened into fragrant wood, and breakfast returned to trembling eggs and handfuls of wheat.

By noon, people carried umbrellas, though the sky was clear. They hid watches in cupboards and wrapped their shoes in newspaper. Only the dogwood outside Mara Vale's house resisted. Each sweep of brightness stopped among its branches and hung there in shining strips. Its white petals closed like small fists, holding the town together for another minute.

Mara was the keeper of the public recipes, a position everyone had mocked until the lighthouse began uncooking the world. She owned instructions for bread, bridges, apologies, and rain. But when she read them aloud toward the tower, the words came apart in her mouth. Flour. Iron. Regret. Cloud. The raw names fell onto the pavement, slick and useless.

At sunset, the dogwood knocked one branch against her window. Mara understood—not the message, but the invitation. She climbed into its crown. The trapped light wrapped around her wrists and lifted her across the square, through the lighthouse wall, into a room smelling of green sap. There was no lamp inside. A vast, unblinking seed rested on a brass pedestal, dreaming the beam outward. Around it, tiny roots searched the floor for soil.

Mara opened her recipe book. Instead of reading, she tore out every page and fed them to the seed. The recipes vanished with soft chewing sounds. Then she cut her palm on the book's bare spine and gave the seed one drop of something no instruction had prepared.

The beam shuddered. Across town, clay remembered roofs, wheat remembered breakfast, and wood tightened itself into music. Yet nothing returned exactly. Chimneys leaned like curious listeners. Spoons retained the taste of rain. Every apology sprouted a white petal.

By morning, the lighthouse had become a narrow dogwood, taller than the clock tower. Mara's old tree stood leafless, its work apparently transferred. Beneath it lay one raw, unwritten page. She carried it home carefully, while all the clocks in town began counting from somewhere else.
