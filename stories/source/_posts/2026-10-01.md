In the town of Vellum, every apple ripened into stone. The orchards rang at noon as branches dropped their fruit onto tin roofs. Children collected the smallest apples for marbles, while bakers displayed empty pies with painted slices in their windows. No one remembered sweetness, though the word remained on everyone’s tongue like a promise too hard to swallow.

A writer named Iven lived above the municipal knife-sharpening shop. Because paper blunted every pen in Vellum, she wrote on steam, breathing against her window and tracing sentences before they vanished. One morning, the mayor brought her an apple the size of a fist. A fine crack circled its green skin.

“Write something soft enough to open it,” he said. “Our hammers have failed.”

Iven carried the apple through town, listening for a suitable sentence. The school bell suggested verbs; laundry flapping on lines supplied commas. At the fountain, water climbed upward in glassy ropes, but refused to offer an ending. All day the apple grew heavier. By dusk, it had stretched her arms toward the pavement, and every unwritten word inside her felt equally dense.

At home, she set the fruit on her desk. Instead of inventing a sentence, Iven confessed one. She breathed upon the window and wrote: I do not know what is inside.

The apple answered with a small sound, not a crack but a sigh. Its shell folded outward in eight neat pages. At the center sat a single brown seed, warm as fresh bread. Iven touched it, and every stone apple in Vellum began to murmur beneath its skin. The noise was neither language nor weather, yet doors opened all along the street.

By morning, roots had threaded through cupboards, clocks, and the mayor’s polished shoes. The hard orchards bent low, offering fruit that bruised under a thumb. People bit cautiously. Juice ran down their wrists, bright and embarrassing.

Iven planted the warm seed in her inkpot. It sprouted a narrow tree whose leaves were blank sheets. From then on, she wrote only questions. Each question ripened, fell, and waited on the floor until someone hungry enough lifted it.
